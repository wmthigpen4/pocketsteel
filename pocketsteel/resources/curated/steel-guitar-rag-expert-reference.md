# Steel Guitar Rag — Expert Reference for Steel Guitar RAG

**Curated content type:** song dossier / history / steel-guitar teaching reference
**Primary topic:** “Steel Guitar Rag”
**Best app behaviors:** answer history questions, identify recordings, explain authorship disputes, provide source links, teach artist performances and complete arrangements in manageable sections, distinguish transcription from adaptation, and label uncertainty rather than inventing exactness.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

“Steel Guitar Rag” is one of the canonical steel-guitar instrumentals in Western swing and country music. The best-known early recording is Bob Wills and His Texas Playboys featuring Leon McAuliffe on steel guitar, recorded in Chicago in 1936 and issued on Vocalion 03394. The tune helped make steel guitar a front-line voice in Western swing rather than merely a Hawaiian or novelty sound.

The app should treat the song as both:

1. **A standard steel-guitar repertoire piece** associated with Leon McAuliffe, Bob Wills, Western swing, and amplified lap steel.
2. **A provenance case study** because its A section is closely related to Sylvester Weaver’s earlier “Guitar Rag,” especially Weaver’s 1927 Okeh recording, and later versions add lyric/composer-credit complications involving Merle Travis and Cliffie Stone.

## 3. Historical Timeline

### 1915 — Hawaiian/vaudeville background

Some discussions connect part of the tune’s ancestry to the Hawaiian hapa-haole song “On the Beach at Waikiki,” credited to Henry Kailimai / G.H. Stover / Sonny Cunha in historical references. Guy Cundell notes McAuliffe himself suggested the B section had a relationship to “On the Beach at Waikiki,” while also observing that the melodic and harmonic relationship is not as direct as the Weaver connection.

### 1923 and 1927 — Sylvester Weaver’s “Guitar Rag”

Sylvester Weaver recorded “Guitar Rag” before McAuliffe’s famous version. Cundell’s analysis identifies Weaver’s 1927 version as the especially strong model for McAuliffe’s A section. SecondHandSongs lists “Steel Guitar Rag” as adapted from “Guitar Rag,” with original music by Sara Martin and Sylvester Weaver.

### 1935 — Leon McAuliffe joins Bob Wills

Cundell states that McAuliffe joined Wills in early 1935 and that the tune had been in the band’s repertoire for roughly eighteen months before the 1936 recording session.

### September 1936 — Bob Wills / Texas Playboys recording

UCSB’s Discography of American Historical Recordings lists Brunswick matrix C1479, “Steel guitar rag,” by the Texas Playboys, recorded in Chicago at the Furniture Mart Building on 9/29/1936. Cundell describes the session as a full-band Western swing recording with steel guitar as the feature instrument.

### 1947 onward — Lyric and vocal versions

Merle Travis recorded a vocal treatment, and modern licensing databases commonly list Cliffie Stone, Leon McAuliffe, and Merle Travis as writers for the song. The app should distinguish between the 1936 instrumental history and later vocal/lyric publishing credits.

## 4. Authorship and Provenance

### Safe, accurate answer

A balanced expert answer should say:

> “Steel Guitar Rag” is commonly associated with Leon McAuliffe and Bob Wills, but its main melodic ancestry is disputed. The famous 1936 Western swing recording closely resembles Sylvester Weaver’s earlier “Guitar Rag,” especially the A section. Later publishing/licensing records often list Leon McAuliffe, Merle Travis, and Cliffie Stone, reflecting later lyric/publishing history rather than resolving the underlying provenance question.

### What not to say

Do not answer simply: “Leon McAuliffe wrote Steel Guitar Rag.” That is incomplete. It erases the Weaver/Guitar Rag relationship.

### What to say instead

Use one of these depending on the question:

- **Beginner answer:** “Leon McAuliffe made it famous with Bob Wills, but the tune is strongly related to Sylvester Weaver’s earlier ‘Guitar Rag.’”
- **Expert answer:** “The 1936 McAuliffe/Wills version is a Western swing steel-guitar adaptation whose A strain closely follows Weaver’s ‘Guitar Rag,’ while the B/C strain, arrangement, electric steel sound, band context, and later lyric/publishing history complicate the authorship story.”

## 5. Musical Form

Cundell describes the McAuliffe composition/arrangement as three distinct 16-bar sections: **A, B, and C**. The 1936 arrangement then includes solo and ensemble returns rather than a simple one-pass statement. The app should know this because many players learn only the head and miss the form.

Recommended teaching breakdown:

1. **Intro / steel glissando** — steel announces the tune as a feature.
2. **A section** — the most recognizable “rag” strain; closest to Weaver’s “Guitar Rag.”
3. **B section** — contrasting strain; often where players add chordal blocking and Western-swing phrasing.
4. **C section** — another contrasting strain; useful for piano/sax/steel solo turns in swing-band versions.
5. **Return / recap** — steel returns to the head.

---

## 6. Instrument and Tuning Context

The original famous recording belongs to the **pre-pedal electric steel** world, not the modern Nashville E9 pedal-steel world. Cundell notes that by the 1936 session McAuliffe was using a Bakelite Rickenbacker B6 steel guitar. That matters because the sound is lap-steel phrasing: bar movement, forward slants/reverse slants, single-note swing phrasing, and chordal stabs.

For the app:

- Treat the original sound as **electric lap steel / non-pedal steel**.
- Treat C6 as a historically natural tuning family for Western swing adaptations.
- Treat E9 pedal-steel versions as later adaptations, not the original idiom.
- When answering a modern E9 player, explain how to approximate the swing phrasing without pretending pedals are required.

## 8. App Answer Patterns

### User asks: “Who wrote Steel Guitar Rag?”

Recommended answer:

> The famous Western swing version is associated with Leon McAuliffe and Bob Wills’ Texas Playboys, but the authorship story is complicated. The 1936 steel-guitar feature is strongly related to Sylvester Weaver’s earlier “Guitar Rag,” especially the A section. Modern licensing records for the song often list Cliffie Stone, Leon McAuliffe, and Merle Travis, reflecting later publishing/lyric history.

### User asks: “Was Steel Guitar Rag stolen?”

Recommended answer:

> The safest expert answer is that it is an adaptation/provenance dispute, not a simple yes/no. Musicologists and discography sources point out a strong relationship between McAuliffe’s 1936 “Steel Guitar Rag” and Sylvester Weaver’s earlier “Guitar Rag.” McAuliffe’s version changed the context, arrangement, steel-guitar execution, and Western swing presentation, but the melodic debt is too strong to ignore.

### User asks: “What tuning was Steel Guitar Rag played on?”

Recommended answer:

> The famous Bob Wills version is a non-pedal electric steel performance, commonly discussed in the lap-steel/Western-swing tradition rather than modern E9 pedal steel. Many modern players adapt it to C6 or E9. For a historically flavored version, C6-style positions usually feel more idiomatic; for a modern pedal-steel player, E9 can work as an adaptation.

### User asks: “Can you give me tab for Steel Guitar Rag?”

Recommended answer:

> Yes. Tell me which recording and section you want to learn, or share a link/upload if I cannot access it. I can teach a faithful transcription, an E9 adaptation, or a simplified arrangement. I will identify the source, label uncertain passages, and divide a complete arrangement into manageable numbered sections.

## 10. E9 Pedal-Steel Teaching Version: Short Original Study

This is **not** a full “Steel Guitar Rag” transcription. It is a short, original E9 exercise that teaches the kind of swing phrasing, open-to-A+B movement, and chordal snap a player can use when approaching the tune.

Assumptions:

- 10-string E9, Emmons setup.
- P1 = A pedal, P2 = B pedal.
- “A+B” means pedals P1 and P2 together.
- Key center: E-style swing vocabulary.
- Pick blocking or palm blocking should keep the eighth notes crisp.

```text
E9 original Western-swing rag study — key of E feel
Not a transcription of Steel Guitar Rag.

     E6/E major flavor                         A move                      E return
F#|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
D#|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
G#|--0B--0B--0B----3B--3B----0B----0B---------------------------------------------|
E |--0---0---0-----3---3-----0-----0----------------------------------------------|
B |--0A--0A--0A----3A--3A----0A----0A-----5A--5A--5A----3A--0A--------------------|
G#|--0B--0B--0B----3B--3B----0B----0B-----5B--5B--5B----3B--0B--------------------|
F#|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
E |--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
D |--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
B |--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
     1  &  2  &     3  &     4  &          1  &  2  &     3  &  4  &
```

Teaching notes:

- The 3rd fret A+B position gives a strong IV-color move against an E home base.
- Keep the line bouncy; do not play it like a straight modern country lick.
- The sound should be short, clear, and slightly percussive.
- For a more period-correct feel, use fewer pedals and more bar movement/chord grips.

## 11. C6 / Non-Pedal Teaching Version: Short Original Study

This is a short C6-flavored exercise for the lap-steel/Western-swing feel. It is not a transcription.

Generic 6-string C6 tuning high-to-low:

```text
1 E
2 C
3 A
4 G
5 E
6 C
```

Original rag-style grip movement:

```text
C6 original Western-swing rag study — not a Steel Guitar Rag transcription

E|--4--4--4----7--7----4----4-------9--9--7--4----------------|
C|--4--4--4----7--7----4----4-------9--9--7--4----------------|
A|--4--4--4----7--7----4----4-------9--9--7--4----------------|
G|-------------------------------------------------------------|
E|-------------------------------------------------------------|
C|-------------------------------------------------------------|
    E-ish home        slide up / answer       snap back
```

Teaching notes:

- Use the bar for vocal-like movement.
- Keep grips tight and muted between attacks.
- Add swing feel by delaying the second eighth note slightly.

Because many versions vary, the app should not force one “official” chord chart unless a source version is selected. For a basic E-major teaching map, describe it as a simplified learning chart:

Listen for:

- Steel-guitar feature role.
- Western swing band support.
- Spoken/bandleader framing.

- Brighter attack.

Listen for:

- Pedal-steel modernization.
- Jazzier substitutions and cleaner technical control.

Listen for:

- Vocal/song version rather than pure instrumental steel feature.

- Travis’s guitar-centric rhythmic feel.
